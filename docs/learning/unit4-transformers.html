You already know three things that make this easy: words become vectors (a3), the dot product measures how similar two vectors are (a3), and RNNs forget over long distances because gradients vanish (a4). A Transformer is what people built to fix that last problem.

1  The big idea

A Transformer lets every word look directly at every other word, all at the same time.

Picture it An RNN is a classroom where a message is whispered from desk to desk down the row. By the last desk the message is blurry: that's the vanishing gradient you measured in a4. A Transformer is a classroom where every student can talk to every other student directly. Nothing has to survive a long chain of whispers, and everyone talks at once, so it's fast too.

Take "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired." To understand it, the word needs to find cat. An RNN has to carry "cat" through 6 steps of whispering. A Transformer lets "it" look straight at "cat" in one step. That act of looking is called attention, and everything in this assignment is built from it.

2  Shapes: the grammar of matrices

A shape (rows × columns) tells you what a matrix holds. Get shapes right and the rest follows.

A sentence of n words, each word a vector of d numbers, is one matrix X of shape (n × d): one row per word. In the assignment d is called d_model.

The only rule you need:   (a × b) @ (b × c)  =  (a × c)
                                ↑       ↑
                          these must match, and they disappear

Example: (3 × 2) @ (2 × 3) = (3 × 3). Whenever you multiply, write the shapes underneath and cancel the middle. The grader explicitly marks "tensor-dimension understanding", so this habit is worth real marks.

Q1Positional encoding: telling words where they are

Attention sees a bag of words with no order, so we add a unique "position signature" to each word.

Attention compares every word with every other word, but nothing in that comparison says which word came first. To attention, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical. We fix it by adding a position vector PE(pos) to each word's vector.

Picture it: a clock A clock has a fast hand (seconds), a medium hand (minutes) and a slow hand (hours). Any one hand repeats. All three together tell you the exact time. Positional encoding is a clock with d/2 hands, each spinning at a different speed. Each pair of dimensions (one sin and one cos) is one hand.

The maths, slowly

PE(pos, 2i)   = sin( pos / 100002i/d )      ← even dimension:  sin
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 100002i/d )      ← odd dimension:   cos

Frequency (how fast hand i spins) is ωi = 1 / 100002i/d. As i grows, the bottom gets bigger, so ω gets smaller: the hand slows down. The wavelength (positions before it repeats) is 2π / ωi = 2π · 100002i/d.

Hand 0 is high-frequency and changes a lot between neighbouring words. Hand 7 is low-frequency and barely moves, which tells you roughly where in a long text you are.

With real numbers (exactly Q1b, d = 4)

d = 4 gives 2 hands: i = 0 divides by 100000 = 1, and i = 1 divides by 100002/4 = 100. So each row is [ sin(pos), cos(pos), sin(pos/100), cos(pos/100) ].

Why this helps with relative position (Q1d)

Moving forward k words turns every hand by a fixed angle, ω·k. Turning a hand is a rotation, and the rotation depends only on k, not on where you started:

[ sin ω(pos+k) ]   [  cos ωk   sin ωk ] [ sin ω·pos ]
[ cos ω(pos+k) ] = [ −sin ωk   cos ωk ] [ cos ω·pos ]

So "3 words later" is the same transformation everywhere in the sentence, and the model can learn "look 3 words back" once and use it anywhere.

Q2Attention: the core idea

Each word asks a question, compares it with every word's label, and takes a mix of their information.

Picture it: a library You walk in with a question ("books about cats?"). That's the Query (Q). Every book has a label on its spine. That's the Key (K). Inside every book is its content. That's the Value (V). You compare your question with every label, decide how much each book matters, and read a mix of the books, mostly from the best matches.

The 4 steps

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT / √dk ) V

Step 1  scores  = Q KT          how well each question matches each label
Step 2  scaled  = scores / √dk    calm the numbers down (explained below)
Step 3  weights = softmax(scaled)   turn each row into percentages that sum to 1
Step 4  output  = weights @ V      take that mix of the contents

Shapes for n words: Q (n × dk) @ KT (dk × n) = (n × n). One score for every pair: row = the word asking, column = the word being looked at. Then weights (n × n) @ V (n × dv) = (n × dv): one new vector per word.

Softmax turns any row of scores into shares: ex makes every number positive (and bigger scores much bigger), then dividing by the row total makes them sum to 1.

(made-up illustration: one row of the n × n weights, summing to 1.00)

Why divide by √dk? (Q2a)

A dot product adds up dk little products. Adding more random numbers makes the total swing wider. If each component of q and k is random with mean 0 and variance 1, then Var(q·k) = dk, so the typical size is √dk. Measured with 20,000 random pairs:

dktypical size of q·k√dkafter ÷ √dk
42.002.001.00

Huge scores make softmax give one word ≈100% and the rest ≈0%, like a teacher who only ever listens to one student. That kills learning (see Q7). Dividing by √dk brings the size back to about 1, whatever d is.

               2.0281/3.0281 = 0.670,   1/3.0281 = 0.330

Step 4  output = weights @ I = the weights themselves
Remember 0.670 / 0.330 is softmax([0.7071, 0]). It comes back in Q3, Q4 and Q6. Learn e0.7071 = 2.0281 once.

Q3Projections: three versions of every word

Each word gets three learned "versions" of itself: one for asking, one for being found, one for giving information.

Picture it Think of yourself on a group project. What you ask others (Query), the skills on your name tag (Key) and what you actually know (Value) are three different things, even though they're all "you". The model learns three matrices that make these three versions. 
Q = X WQ     K = X WK     V = X WV
(n × d_model) @ (d_model × dk) = (n × dk)        WV gives (n × dv)

WQ and WK must have the same output size dk, because Q and K get dot-producted together. WV can have a different size dv. They differ because asking, being found and giving information are different jobs, so each needs its own learned view of the word.

The surprise in Q3d

With identity matrices the scores are [[5,2],[2,1]]. With the supplied matrices they are [[4,1],[1,0]]. Every row went down by exactly 1. Softmax only cares about differences inside a row: if everyone in a class gets 5 bonus marks, the ranking doesn't change. So the weights come out identical (0.893 / 0.107 and 0.670 / 0.330). Only the output changes, because WV doubled the first column of V.

Most students will write "the projections changed who attends to whom". For these matrices that is wrong, and you'll show why. (In general projections do change the weights; the notebook proves it with a random WK.)

Q4The causal mask: no peeking at the future

When generating text, a word may only look at itself and the words before it.

Picture it Writing a story one word at a time, you can't read words you haven't written yet. In training we feed the model the whole sentence at once, so we must hide the future, like sliding a card down a quiz sheet. Without it the model would just copy the next word, which is cheating.

We add a mask M to the scores before softmax: 0 where looking is allowed (j ≤ i), −∞ where it isn't (j > i). Since e−∞ = 0, blocked words get exactly 0 weight.

Word 1 can only see itself, so it gets 100%. Notice row 2 is our old friend 0.330 / 0.670.

Causal vs padding mask (Q4d). Sentences in a batch have different lengths, so short ones are filled with blank "padding" tokens. A padding mask blocks those blank columns for everyone, so no word looks at filler. A causal mask blocks the future, the triangle above the diagonal. Different reasons, same trick (−∞ before softmax).

Q5Cross-attention: looking at another sentence

Same attention, but the questions come from one sentence and the labels and contents from another.

Picture it: a translator Translating मलाई चिया मन पर्छ (malai chiya man parchha) into "I like tea": each time you write an English word, you glance back at the Nepali sentence to see which Nepali words matter right now. The English side asks (Q); the Nepali side answers (K and V). 
Q = Hdecoder WQ     K = Hencoder WK     V = Hencoder WV
weights: (n decoder words × dk) @ (dk × m encoder words) = (n × m)

Self-attention: one sentence looks at itself, so the weights are n × n, square. Cross-attention: 3 English words looking at 4 Nepali words gives 3 × 4, not square, because the two sentences have different lengths. Each row still sums to 1.

With real numbers (Q5b)

QKT = [1, 0, 1]   ÷√2 → [0.7071, 0, 0.7071]
e: 2.0281, 1, 2.0281   total 5.0562   → weights [0.401, 0.198, 0.401]
output = 0.401·[2,0] + 0.198·[0,2] + 0.401·[1,1] = [1.203, 0.797]

Q6Multi-head attention: several teammates looking for different things

Split each word's vector into H smaller pieces, run attention on each piece separately, then glue the results back together.

Picture it One attention pattern can only look for one kind of relationship at a time. So use a team: one teammate tracks grammar, one tracks who-did-what, one watches nearby words. Each head is a teammate with its own WQ, WK, WV. At the end their notes are put side by side (concat) and WO blends them into one report.

The two toy heads (Q6b)

Head 1 uses identity matrices, so it is exactly Q2b: weights [[0.670, 0.330], [0.330, 0.670]]. Head 2 uses the swap matrix P for both WQ and WK. Swapping both sides cancels out: QKT = X P PT XT = X XT because P PT = I. So head 2 gives the same weights as head 1. Concat puts them side by side: (2 × 2) + (2 × 2) → (2 × 4). That's wider than d_model = 2, and bringing it back to d_model is exactly WO's job.

Do more heads mean more power? (Q6d) Not necessarily.

  • Same pizza, more slices. Total size is fixed: H × dk = d_model. For d_model = 8 the parameter count is 256 whether H is 1, 2, 4 or 8. More heads don't add parameters.
  • Thinner slices. Each head works in only dk dimensions, so its score matrix can express at most dk "independent directions" (rank ≤ dk). With 8 heads of size 1, each head is very limited.
  • Copycats. Heads can end up doing the same job, like our two toy heads. Research found many heads in trained models can be removed with little loss (Michel et al. 2019; Voita et al. 2019).

Q7Keeping softmax safe, and temperature

Subtracting the biggest score before exponentiating changes nothing mathematically but stops the computer overflowing.

The biggest number a computer float can hold is about e709. So e1000 becomes infinity, and ∞ / ∞ is nan ("not a number"). Softmax of [1000, 1001, 1002] done directly gives [nan, nan, nan].

The proof (Q7a): it's one cancellation

softmax(z − c)i = ezi−c / Σ ezj−c
                = e−c·ezi / ( e−c·Σ ezj )     e−c is the same in every term
                = ezi / Σ ezj  =  softmax(z)i          it cancels top and bottom ∎

Pick c = max(z): the biggest exponent becomes e0 = 1 and nothing can overflow. For [1000, 1001, 1002]: subtract 1002 → [−2, −1, 0] → e: 0.1353, 0.3679, 1 → [0.090, 0.245, 0.665]. (This is the same cancellation that made Q3d's weights identical.)

Temperature T: how sure the softmax is

Divide scores by T before softmax. Small T = sharp (one winner). Big T = flat (everyone similar). Scores [2, 1, 0]:

Tword 1word 2word 3feels like
0.50.8670.1170.016very sure, almost one winner
10.6650.2450.090normal (Q7b's numbers again!)
20.5060.3070.186relaxed
40.4190.3260.254almost no preference

Dividing by √dk in attention is exactly using T = √dk.

Why "too sure" stops learning (Q7d)

How much output i moves when score j moves is ∂si/∂zj = si(δij − sj), where δij is 1 if i = j and 0 otherwise. If one s is ≈ 1 and the rest ≈ 0, every term is ≈ 0, so the gradient vanishes. Scores [2, 1, 0] give a biggest entry of 0.223; scores [20, 1, 0] give 0.0000000077. A student who is 100% sure never changes their mind. That's why we scale by √dk: to keep softmax out of that zone.

Q8The encoder block: talk, then think

Every word first talks to the others (attention), then thinks alone (feed-forward), tidying up after each step.

Picture it A study group. Step 1, discuss: everyone shares with everyone (attention). Step 2, think alone: each student quietly processes what they heard (feed-forward network). After each step: keep your original notes and add the new ones (residual), then tidy up (LayerNorm). 
H = LayerNorm( X + MultiHead(X) )
Y = LayerNorm( H + FFN(H) )          FFN(H) = ReLU(H W1 + b1) W2 + b2
PartWhat it does, simply
AttentionMixes information between words. Change one word and every row changes.
Feed-forwardThe same small network applied to each word on its own: d → d_ff (wider, usually 4×) → ReLU → d. Change word 2 and only row 2 changes.
Residual (+)Add the input back: x + F(x). Its gradient is I + ∂F/∂x, and that I is a shortcut lane, so the gradient always gets through even if ∂F/∂x is tiny. It's the fix for a4's vanishing gradients.
LayerNormFor each word separately, rescale its d numbers to mean 0 and spread 1 (like turning marks into z-scores), then let learned γ and β rescale: γ·(x − μ)/√(σ² + ε) + β.

Q9The decoder, and the whole Transformer

The decoder writes the output one word at a time: look at what you've written (masked), look at the source (cross), think, then pick the next word.

A decoder block has three sub-layers, each followed by Add & Norm:

  1. Masked self-attention: look at the words written so far, never the future (Q4).
  2. Cross-attention: Q from here, K and V from the encoder's output: "which source words matter now?" (Q5).
  3. Feed-forward: think alone (Q8).

Trace one translation (Q9c)

Source words → embedding + PE → N encoder blocks → encoder output (m × d) → used as K and V in every decoder block's cross-attention → decoder output → Linear → softmax → the most likely next word is picked, appended to the output, and the decoder runs again.

Why the decoder needs the mask but the encoder doesn't: the encoder reads a sentence that already exists in full, so every word may see every other word. The decoder produces words one at a time. At generation time the future doesn't exist yet, so in training it must be hidden too, or the model learns to cheat.

Q10The cost: why long texts are expensive

Every word looks at every word, so n words means n × n pairs: double the text, four times the attention work.

OperationOne headAll H heads (H·dk = d)
Q, K, V projections3 · n · d · dk3 · n · d²
Scores Q KTn² · dkn² · d
Mix: weights @ Vn² · dvn² · d
Attention matrix memoryn²H · n²

Memory for H = 4 (8 bytes per number): n = 32 → 32 KiB · 64 → 128 KiB · 128 → 512 KiB · 256 → 2 MiB · 512 → 8 MiB. The text got 16× longer; memory grew 256× (= 16²).

Remember: the crossover (your Q10 insight) Projections (plus WO) cost about 4n·d²; attention costs about 2n²·d. They're equal when n = 2d: 128 for d = 64. Below 128 the projections dominate, so time grows slower than n². Above 128, n² takes over. So your timing curve will start gentle and steepen.

Masking: plain code still computes the whole n × n square and then blanks the triangle, so it saves no arithmetic. Only smarter implementations skip the blocked half. That's the difference between arithmetic cost (the maths) and implementation savings (clever code). Heads: more heads keep the arithmetic the same (H·dk = d) but need H separate n × n matrices in memory.

If you're picked to present

Ten questions you're likely to get, each with a two-line answer. If you can say these in your own words, you're ready.

1. What problem does attention solve?
RNNs pass information step by step and forget over long distances (vanishing gradients). Attention lets every word look at every other word directly, in one step, all in parallel.
2. What are Q, K and V?
Three learned versions of each word: the Query is what it's looking for, the Key is what it offers to be found by, the Value is the information it hands over.
3. Why divide by √dk?
Dot products grow like √dk. Big scores push softmax to ≈100% for one word, where gradients vanish. Dividing keeps scores around size 1.
4. Why positional encoding?
Attention has no sense of order: "dog bites man" = "man bites dog". Sin/cos waves at different speeds give each position a unique signature, and a shift of k is always the same rotation.
5. Why a causal mask in the decoder but not the encoder?
The decoder generates word by word, so it must not see the future, or it learns to cheat. The encoder reads a complete sentence, so seeing everything is fine.
6. Cross-attention vs self-attention?
Self: Q, K, V from the same sentence, square n × n weights. Cross: Q from the decoder, K and V from the encoder, n × m weights; that's how the decoder reads the source.
7. Why multiple heads, and do more always help?
Different heads can track different relationships. But the total size is fixed (H·dk = d), each head gets thinner, and heads can be redundant: our two toy heads were identical.
8. Why subtract the max in softmax?
e1000 overflows to infinity and gives nan. Subtracting a constant changes nothing (it cancels top and bottom) but makes the biggest exponent e0 = 1.
9. What do residuals and LayerNorm do?
A residual adds the input back, so the gradient has a shortcut (I + ∂F/∂x) and doesn't vanish. LayerNorm rescales each word's vector to mean 0 and spread 1, so training stays stable.
10. Why is attention O(n²), and why does it matter?
Every word scores against every word: n² pairs, plus an n × n matrix per head in memory. Doubling the text quadruples that cost, which is why long documents are expensive.
Three findings that will impress Q3d: the supplied projections give the same attention weights as identity, because every score row shifted by a constant and softmax ignores that. · Q6b: the two heads are identical because P PT = I, which is live proof that more heads ≠ more capacity. · Q10: n² only takes over past n = 2·d_model = 128; below that the projections cost more.
