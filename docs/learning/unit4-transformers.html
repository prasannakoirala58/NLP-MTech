The handwritten notes have all the maths. This page is only the story: what problem each piece solves, and what each calculation means. Read one question, pause, then the next.

The whole Transformer in 5 sentences
  1. Every word becomes a list of numbers (you did this in a3).
  2. Every word gets a tag saying where it sits in the sentence. (Q1)
  3. Every word looks at all the other words and decides which ones matter to it. (Q2–Q7)
  4. It gathers information from the ones that matter, thinks about it, tidies up, and repeats. (Q8)
  5. At the end, the model picks the most likely next word. (Q9)

Q1Positional encoding: where is each word?

The problem. A Transformer reads all the words at the same time, like word cards dumped on a table. Nothing tells it which card came first, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look the same to it.

The fix. Before anything else, stamp every word with a little "where am I" tag.

Picture it: lamps on cinema seats Every seat in a cinema row has 3 little lamps. Lamp A flips on/off every seat. Lamp B flips every 2 seats. Lamp C flips every 4 seats. Look at the table: every seat gets its own pattern. No two seats look the same, so the pattern is the seat's address.
seat01234567
Lamp A (fast)○●○●○●○●
Lamp B (medium)○○●●○○●●
Lamp C (slow)○○○○●●●●

every column is a different pattern, so every seat has a unique address

So where do sine and cosine come in?

Real position tags use exactly this idea, with two upgrades:

  • Dimmer lamps instead of on/off switches. Each lamp glows brighter and darker in a smooth, steady wave. That's all sine and cosine are: smooth waves that go up and down between −1 and +1, forever, at a steady rhythm.
  • Many more lamps, each with its own speed: some fast, some very slow.

Fast lamps tell next-door words apart (word 5 vs word 6). Slow lamps tell far-apart parts of the text apart (word 5 vs word 500). Together, every position gets a unique tag.

Why a sine AND a cosine? A Ferris wheel If I only tell you how high your cabin is, you could be going up or coming down: same height, two different places. If I also tell you how far left or right it is, you know exactly where you are. Sine = the height, cosine = left/right. That's why each lamp is really a sine + cosine pair.

What is the 10000? It's the dial that sets how slow the slowest lamp is. 10000 makes it so slow that it never comes back round to the same brightness in any real text, so two far-apart positions can never be confused.

Why it helps with "relative position". Moving 3 words along always turns every lamp by the same amount, wherever you start. So the model can learn "look at the word 3 back" once and use it anywhere in the sentence.

What you'll calculate (Q1b) The brightness of 4 lamps (2 fast, 2 slow) for words 0, 1 and 2: twelve numbers in a small table. You'll see the idea in them: the fast lamps change a lot from word to word, the slow lamps barely move.
Remember Positional encoding = a unique "where am I" tag made of fast and slow waves, added to each word.

Q2Attention: who should I listen to?

The problem. A word alone is ambiguous. In "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired", the word "it" only makes sense once you know it means the cat.

Picture it: a new student asking for help A new student is stuck on a maths question.
  • They know what they need: help with maths. That's the Query (Q).
  • Everyone in class has a reputation: "good at maths", "good at football". That's the Key (K).
  • Everyone also has the actual knowledge they'd share. That's the Value (V).
The student compares their need with every reputation, decides how much to listen to each person, and blends what they hear.
What we calculate, in words
  1. Match scores: how well my question fits each word's reputation. Big score = good match.
  2. Calm the scores down: comparing long lists of numbers gives big scores just because the lists are long. Without calming down, the word would listen to one single word and ignore everyone else. So we divide by a number that grows with the list length (√d).
  3. Softmax: turn the scores into percentages that add up to 100%. Bigger score, bigger share.
  4. Blend: take each word's information in proportion to its share.
"it" listens to →Thecatsatbecauseitwastired
share5%70%10%5%5%3%2%

made-up example: one row of attention, adding up to 100%

Remember Attention = "who should I listen to, and how much?" The result is a table of percentages, one row per word.

Q3Q, K, V: three versions of every word

The problem. Each word plays three different roles: asking, being found and giving information. One version of the word can't be good at all three jobs.

Picture it: a job portal On a job portal, what you search for (Query), your profile headline (Key) and your actual skills (Value) are three different things, even though they're all "you".
What we calculate Each word is passed through three learned "lenses" (three tables of numbers, WQ, WK, WV) to make its three versions. Then we run attention as in Q2.

The surprise in Q3d. With the lenses the assignment gives, every match score in a row goes down by the same amount. That's like every student getting 5 bonus marks: the ranking doesn't change. So who-listens-to-whom stays exactly the same, and only what gets passed along changes. Most students will miss this.

Remember Q, K, V are three learned versions of each word: for asking, for being found, for sharing.

Q4The causal mask: no peeking at the future

The problem. When a model writes text, it writes one word at a time and can't use words that don't exist yet. But in training we show it whole sentences, so it could cheat by peeking at the next word.

Picture it Your phone's autocomplete can only use what you've typed so far. Or a quiz where you cover the answers with a card and slide it down one line at a time.
What we calculate The usual match scores, then every score for a future word is set to "minus infinity". Softmax turns minus infinity into exactly 0%. So word 1 sees only itself, word 2 sees words 1–2, and so on: a staircase.

the staircase: dark = hidden future

A different mask: padding. Sentences in a batch get filled with blank "filler" words so they all have the same length. A padding mask tells everyone to ignore the blank seats. Causal mask = hide the future. Padding mask = hide the blanks.

Remember The causal mask is a blindfold over the future, so the model can't cheat while learning to write.

Q5Cross-attention: looking at another sentence

The problem. When translating, the writer has to look at the original sentence, not only at what it has written so far.

Picture it: translating Translating मलाई चिया मन पर्छ into "I like tea". When you write "tea", you glance back at the Nepali sentence and focus on चिया.
What we calculate The same attention as Q2, with one change: the questions come from the sentence being written (the decoder), and the reputations and knowledge come from the original sentence (the encoder).

Why the table isn't square. 3 English words asking about 4 Nepali words gives a 3-by-4 table. Two different sentences, two different lengths. In self-attention a sentence looks at itself, so the table is always square.

Remember Self-attention = a sentence looking at itself. Cross-attention = the translation looking at the original.

Q6Multi-head attention: a team instead of one reader

The problem. One attention pattern can only follow one kind of relationship at a time.

Picture it: checking a contract as a team One person checks names and dates, one checks the money, one checks the grammar. Each reads the whole contract but looks for different things. At the end they put their notes together.
What we calculate Split each word's numbers into equal pieces, one per head (teammate). Each head runs its own attention with its own lenses. Put the results side by side (concatenate). One final table (WO) blends them into one answer.

Do more heads always help? No.

  • Same pizza, more slices. The total size never changes. More heads just means each head gets a thinner slice to work with.
  • Copycats. Heads can end up doing the same job. In the assignment's toy example the two heads come out identical.
Remember Heads = teammates looking for different things, sharing one fixed budget.

Q7Keeping softmax safe, and the confidence knob

Problem 1: huge numbers break computers. Softmax raises e to the power of each score. e to the power 1000 is too big for a computer: it becomes "infinity", then "not a number", and everything breaks.

The fix: measure from the tallest person Instead of everyone's height above the ground, measure everyone's height relative to the tallest person. The differences, which are all softmax cares about, stay exactly the same, but now the numbers are small and safe. So we subtract the biggest score from every score first. Same answer, no explosion.

Problem 2: how confident should attention be? Temperature is a confidence knob:

  • Low temperature: decisive, gives almost everything to one word.
  • High temperature: undecided, spreads attention evenly.

Why "too confident" stops learning. A student who is 100% sure doesn't change their mind when corrected, so they stop learning. Softmax is the same: when it's nearly 100% on one word, small changes to the scores change nothing, so the model gets no learning signal. Dividing by √d (Q2) keeps it in the healthy middle.

Remember Subtract the max to stay safe. Keep confidence moderate to keep learning.

Q8The encoder block: talk, then think

Picture it: a study group's routine 1. Discuss: everyone shares with everyone (attention). 2. Keep your notes and add what you heard (residual). 3. Tidy up (layer norm). 4. Think alone (feed-forward). 5. Add and tidy again.
  • Residual (the "add"). Like editing an essay while keeping the original draft: you add improvements on top instead of rewriting from scratch. It also gives the learning signal a shortcut lane back to the start, which fixes the vanishing problem you saw in RNNs (a4).
  • Layer norm (the "tidy"). Brings each word's numbers to a standard scale, like converting marks "out of 50" and "out of 100" into percentages, so nothing gets wildly big or small.
  • Feed-forward vs attention. Attention = talking to others (mixes words). Feed-forward = thinking alone (each word on its own). Change one word: attention's output changes for every word; feed-forward's output changes only for that word.
Remember Talk → add → tidy → think → add → tidy. The shape never changes: words in, same number of words out.

Q9The decoder, and the whole Transformer

Picture it: a translation office The reader (encoder) reads the whole Nepali sentence and makes detailed notes. The writer (decoder) writes the English one word at a time. For each new word the writer:
  1. re-reads what they've written so far, never ahead (masked attention, Q4)
  2. checks the reader's notes (cross-attention, Q5)
  3. thinks it over (feed-forward, Q8)
  4. scores every word in the English dictionary and picks the most likely one (linear + softmax)
Then they repeat for the next word.

Why only the decoder needs the mask. The reader has the complete sentence in front of them: there's no future to hide. The writer is creating the sentence, and the next words don't exist yet.

Remember The encoder reads. The decoder writes, looking back at its own words and at the encoder's notes.

Q10The cost: why long texts are slow

Picture it: handshakes In a room where everyone greets everyone, 10 people means 100 greetings and 20 people means 400. Double the people, four times the greetings. Attention is exactly this: every word with every word.

pairs to compare1,0244,09616,38465,536262,144

the text gets 16× longer, the work gets 256× bigger

  • The twist. For short texts, preparing the three versions of each word (Q, K, V) costs more than the handshakes. The handshakes only take over after about 128 words (with the assignment's settings). So the timing graph starts gentle and gets steeper.
  • Masking. Simple code still calculates the whole table and then hides half, so no time is saved. Only clever code skips the hidden half.
  • More heads. Same total work, but each head keeps its own table in memory.
Remember Double the text, four times the attention work. That's why long documents are expensive.
